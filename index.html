Welcome to my homepage for DMS4. This page hosts the assignments for Interface design and development produced throughout the semester. Assignment 3 is featured here on the homepage.

My project focuses on balancing music creation with the ability to listen to it mindlessly in the background on a personal device. I selected the dynamic soundtrack-er, which focuses on features that let users create and listen to music rather than solely producing it. The prototype consists of a piano keyboard, featuring all keys of a single octave, C to C. Additional features are housed in the top bar section of the interface and are kept as simple and user-friendly as possible. Paired with the modal, the site communicates its intentions through the subheading and modal description. “Build a calm loop to study to”, suggesting to the user both the creativity and the idle aspect of the site.

By actively responding to my design scenario, my project naturally took the shape of communicating how I could express its functions through the layout. I expect the first feature furthest to the left, ‘Presets’, to be the most frequently used, as it is a gateway to ‘fast-create’ content. I used some popular example loops such as ‘Minecraft Theme by C418’, an original and classic piece which is ideal to use for study music. A future ambition would be to turn ‘Presets’ into ‘Import & saved’, as it would respond to the dynamic soundtrack-er scenario I had chosen. ‘Play & record’ is the main course of the site, as it supports the study factor that is intended to be used. Although the creation aspect of the music retains a bit of friction, it encourages users to give it a try and then move on to using presets, so that feature does not get dismissed. The four remaining controls enhance the flexibility and variety for the user to experiment with. ‘Loop speed’ changes how fast the playback is, providing preference for the users. The remaining three controls, ‘Octaves’, ‘Master volume’, and ‘Playmode’, allow the user to adjust the output of the sound as they please. Subtly placed to the right of the ‘sound visualiser’, there are four white crosses. When hovered over, they turn red and can be used to remove sections of recorded notes. This was strategically placed to be easily missed so that users can focus on the visuals at the centre of the screen. Another future feature I’d like to add that would further boost the clarity of the interface is a quick and seamless tutorial that feels almost invisible.

Some extended techniques that I’ve implemented into the interface included some intentional ones as well as some that weren’t so deliberate. One of the unintended techniques was swiping the keys and expecting them to play. While coding, the way the buttons are designed is by using ‘onmousedown’, so I drew the keyboard in an arc using trigonometry and Claude’s assistance to align with the user’s behavioural expectations. Following this, the looping function and the timing of the playhead and playhead-blocks align well with the dynamic part of the instrument.

I’ve attempted to really balance the use aspect and the idle aspect of the site. The major challenge was finding where dynamic suits the interface the most. “Was it supposed to be more interactive?” “Can it be more subtle and less distracting?” There were many questions I asked myself as I built towards the final instrument. The instrument responds to ‘dynamic soundtrack-er’ by offering the best of both worlds. By simply toggling the play button and then shifting to another tab on the device, the sounds from the instrument will continue to play in a loop. This technical feature pushes the interface beyond its boundaries and lets it feel dynamic in the sense that “I have create/imported a piece of music of my choice, and now I can listen to it as I do other things.”
